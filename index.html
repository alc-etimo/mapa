Inclusive Communication

Making sure every voice is heard — and every identity is preserved.

Inclusive communication is about more than accessibility — it's about representation, identity and belonging. Every project in this section shows how I approach translation and localization with empathy, cultural awareness and a commitment to preserving what makes each voice unique.

Case 01 Accessibility & Identity

Preserving identity through accessibility.

The challenge

Closed captions are essential for accessibility, but they often simplify or erase sociolinguistic markers — accents, dialects, speech patterns — that are fundamental to character identity and representation.

What I observed

In many closed captions, linguistic identity markers are treated as errors to be corrected, rather than features to be preserved.

My approach

Instead of asking "Can the audience understand this?", I asked "What disappears when we simplify it?"

The solution

I developed a practical framework that distinguishes between actual errors (which should be corrected) and identity markers (which should be preserved).

📸 The difference is visible

Two captions from the same scene — one erases identity, the other preserves it.

Before: Identity Erased

❌ Identity erased. The viewer learns nothing about the speaker's background.

After: Identity Preserved

✅ Identity preserved. The viewer understands who is speaking — and why it matters.

📋 Decision Protocol: Error vs. Identity Marker

Treat as ERROR
  • • Grammatical mistakes
  • • Typos / misspellings
  • • Incoherent sentences
  • • Inaccurate information
Preserve as IDENTITY MARKER
  • • Regional accents / dialects
  • • Sociolects / class markers
  • • Speech patterns / rhythm
  • • Cultural expressions

The key insight: Not every deviation from standard language is an error. Sometimes, it's a story.

Case 02 Audiovisual Localization

Helping stories cross borders.

The challenge

Independent productions rarely have the resources of large studios. Their scripts, subtitles and promotional materials must be translated with the same care as the films themselves.

What I observed

Literal translation isn't enough. Every cultural reference, dialect, joke, silence and emotional nuance contributes to how audiences experience the story.

My approach

I begin by understanding what the filmmaker wants the audience to feel — not simply what each sentence means. I adapt dialogue, rhythm, and terminology while preserving each character's identity.

The solution

I delivered localized subtitles, translated scripts and accessibility assets that respected technical standards while remaining emotionally natural — making audiences forget they are reading subtitles.

Case 01 Operational Intelligence

Excel & Google Sheets: daily operational tool.

The challenge

At TeamWorker / Claro, I needed to transform raw editorial quality data into actionable insights for stakeholders, while maintaining a scalable and consistent QA tracking system.

What I observed

Raw QA data was scattered and inconsistent. Stakeholders needed clear, visual reports to make decisions. The system needed to be both rigorous for QA and intuitive for non-technical audiences.

My approach

I built a centralized QA tracker in Excel and Google Sheets, categorizing errors by type (grammar, formatting, taxonomy, plain-language), and used formulas, pivot tables and filtered views to extract monthly indicators.

The solution

I turned raw QA rows into stakeholder-ready dashboards, training plans and monthly reports — enabling data-driven decisions and targeted writer improvement.

Case 02 People & Process

Structuring onboarding & training for quality at scale.

The challenge

New linguists and writers arrive with different levels of experience and familiarity with editorial standards. Without a structured process, quality varies and ramp-up time is unpredictable.

What I observed

Without clear onboarding and training, the cost of quality is always higher. Teams waste time on avoidable errors, and stakeholders lose confidence in deliverables.

My approach

I designed a structured onboarding flow with clear stages: Test → Evaluation → Approval → Registration → Permissions → Onboarding. Every new team member follows the same path, ensuring consistency from day one.

The solution

A replicable onboarding process that ensures every new linguist understands the editorial standards, system permissions and quality expectations before they start producing — reducing errors and accelerating time-to-productivity.

Case 01 Knowledge Architecture & AI

Making complex knowledge easy to navigate.

The challenge

During a large-scale migration of more than 15,000 knowledge articles into Salesforce, customer support agents were struggling to locate information quickly enough during live calls.

What I observed

Articles contained multiple customer scenarios inside long, linear documentation. Average handling time targets were below four minutes. Reading everything wasn't realistic.

My approach

Instead of optimizing documentation, I redesigned the navigation model itself. Working alongside Process Engineering teams, I translated complex ARIS decision trees into guided user journeys designed for real call center behaviour.

The solution

I designed a structured decision-tree framework based on anchors, conditional navigation and standardized content architecture. The model was validated by IBM engineering and became the company's new standard for interactive knowledge articles.

Case 02 AI Evaluation & Linguistics

Teaching AI to communicate better.

The challenge

Modern language models fail because they misunderstand context, culture, ambiguity, user intent and conversational expectations. Those failures are often subtle, making them difficult to identify consistently.

What I observed

AI responses often sound fluent but are logically broken. Fluency ≠ precision. My linguistic background allows me to distinguish grammatical quality from communicative quality.

My approach

I evaluate responses the same way I evaluate human communication. Is the information grounded? Is the reasoning coherent? Does the answer actually solve the user's problem?

The solution

Across multiple AI evaluation projects, I have annotated, ranked, rewritten and documented model behavior, producing detailed rationales that help improve future model performance. Reproducible evaluation criteria are the goal.

🧠 From prompt to evaluation

How I evaluate model responses — and why it matters.

Prompt
User: "How do I reset my password if I forgot it?"

Simple question · Multiple possible answers

Response A (AI)
"To reset your password, please click on the 'Forgot Password' link. You will receive an email with a link to reset your password. If you don't receive it, check your spam folder. Then, create a new password and confirm it. You're all set!"

❌ Fluent but generic · Doesn't consider user's context

Response B + My Evaluation
"To reset your password, go to the login page and click 'Forgot Password'. Follow the instructions sent to your registered email. If you're using a company account, contact your IT admin."
✅ My reasoning: More helpful than A — accounts for enterprise context.
⚠️ Could add: "If you're locked out, use the recovery option."
